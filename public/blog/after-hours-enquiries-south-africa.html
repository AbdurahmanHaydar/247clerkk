The after-hours enquiry: what South African practices lose overnight

247clerk · Published 19 August 2026 · Updated 19 August 2026 · 7 min read

Short answer

A large share of enquiries to professional practices arrive outside office hours, because that is when the person finally has time to deal with the thing that has been worrying them. Left unanswered until morning, a meaningful proportion go elsewhere — not out of impatience, but because a worried person keeps messaging until somebody replies. The fix is not longer hours; it is an immediate, useful reply in the channel the enquiry arrived in.

Every practice knows the pattern anecdotally. The messages are timestamped 20:40, 22:15, 23:50. Sunday afternoon is busier than Tuesday morning. Nobody plans for it, because the practice's operating model assumes enquiries arrive when the office is open.

Why enquiries cluster at night

It is not that clients prefer inconvenient hours. It is that the enquiry is usually the second thing that happened.

Someone opens a letter from SARS at 18:30. An offer to purchase comes back signed at 21:00. A visa rejection arrives by email during the working day, and the person spends the afternoon absorbing it before they can face doing something about it. A tenant is served at the weekend. In each case the trigger lands outside the window in which anything can be done about it, and the enquiry follows within a few hours — often late, often anxious, and almost always by message rather than by call, because messaging at 23:00 feels reasonable and phoning does not.

This has a consequence practices consistently underrate: the after-hours enquiry is disproportionately urgent. These are not idle browsers. Something happened today. A deadline is frequently already running.

What actually happens to a 23:00 enquiry

Walk the sequence honestly for an unattended practice:

  1. 23:00 — the message arrives on the firm's WhatsApp. No reply.
  2. 23:10 — the person, still holding their phone and still worried, searches again and messages two more practices.
  3. 23:20 — one of them replies. It may be a competitor with a clerk on duty; it may be an attorney who happened to be awake.
  4. 08:30 — you reply. The client has spoken to someone else, has been given a time and a price, and is now comparing you to a firm that answered.

The lost instruction is rarely lost at 08:30. It was lost at 23:20, and the morning reply is a formality. Research on lead response across industries consistently finds the same thing: the practice that responds first wins a substantially disproportionate share, and the advantage decays within minutes rather than hours.

What one missed enquiry is actually worth

This is worth doing with your own numbers rather than accepting a vendor's arithmetic. The structure:

PracticeTypical first instructionWhat one lost enquiry a month costs annually
ConveyancingA transferTwelve transfers of fees — before the referrals each would have produced
Deceased estatesAn executorshipTwelve estates, each running months and generating adjacent work
ImmigrationAn application or appealTwelve matters, in a practice area where clients refer heavily within communities
Tax disputesAn objection or disputeTwelve engagements, frequently recurring annually thereafter
Estate agencyA mandateTwelve mandates — the commission on one typically dwarfs a year of any software

The point is not the precise figure. It is that for fee-earning practices, the cost of missing enquiries is measured in matters, while the cost of covering them is measured in hundreds of rands a month. The two sit in different orders of magnitude, which is why the decision usually only looks difficult until someone writes it down.

The four ways practices cover the gap

Voicemail and an auto-reply

"Thank you for your message, we will respond during office hours." This is not coverage; it is an acknowledgement that you are not there, delivered instantly. It does nothing for the client at 23:10 who is already messaging your competitors. Its one virtue is honesty.

Somebody's phone

The most common arrangement in small practices: a partner or candidate attorney answers when they see it. It works, sporadically, and it has three problems. It depends on one person being awake and inclined; it puts client conversations on a personal handset with all the POPIA consequences that follow (see WhatsApp and POPIA for law firms); and it burns the goodwill of the person doing it. Practices rarely admit how much intake rests on one conscientious junior.

An after-hours answering service

A human service takes the call and sends a message. Genuine judgement, genuine warmth. The limits are that it is priced per call or per minute, it is usually voice-only in a market whose enquiries arrive by message, and the operator cannot screen a legal matter — you get "Mr Naidoo called about a property thing", which is a note, not intake.

An automated clerk on the messaging channel

Answers the message in seconds, handles the fee and process questions, screens the matter against your criteria, offers real diary slots and takes the consultation fee. The trade-off is honest: it is not a person, and for a regulated practice it must be built to refuse to advise and to escalate anything touching the merits. Where it wins is that it is available at 23:00 and consistent at 03:00, which no person is.

What "covered" should actually mean

Coverage is not "someone eventually responds". A good after-hours reply does four things before the client puts the phone down:

  • Acknowledges the specific thing they said, not their message in the abstract. "Appeals are our area" beats "we have received your enquiry".
  • Answers the question they actually asked — almost always cost, timeline, or whether you handle this at all.
  • Establishes urgency. Is a deadline running? That single question changes how the morning is triaged.
  • Ends with a commitment. A booked time, ideally confirmed by a paid fee. Anything less leaves the client still shopping.

A reply that does all four converts at a completely different rate from one that does the first. It is also, not coincidentally, exactly what a good human clerk would have done.

Measure it before you fix it

Before buying anything, spend twenty minutes on last month's data:

  1. Count enquiries that arrived outside 08:00–17:00, weekends included.
  2. Count how many got a reply within an hour.
  3. Count how many became instructions.
  4. Multiply the gap by your average first-matter value.

Most practices are surprised twice: by how much of their intake arrives after hours, and by how much better the conversion rate is on the few that happened to get a quick reply. That second number is the argument.
